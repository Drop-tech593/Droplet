How Text Extraction Works

A PDF can store text in two fundamentally different ways. Understanding the difference explains most of what you will see in the output.

Text-based PDFs (created by Word, Google Docs, LaTeX, or any "Save as PDF" export) contain a real text layer. Each character is stored as a glyph with a position, a font reference, and an encoding. This tool reads that layer directly using PDF.js and joins the characters into words and lines. This is fast and accurate.

Scanned PDFs are photographs of paper. There is no text layer — just pixels. To get text out of them, the tool renders each page as an image and runs OCR (Optical Character Recognition) on it. This is slower and less accurate than reading a real text layer, but it is the only way to recover the words.

Page-by-Page Decision

The converter does not decide "this whole PDF is scanned" or "this whole PDF is text". It decides per page. For each page, it first tries the embedded text layer. If the extracted text looks meaningful — at least 80 characters, 40 letters, and 10 words of real content — it uses that text. Otherwise, it falls back to OCR for that page only.

This matters for mixed documents. A contract where page 1 is a typed cover sheet and pages 2–5 are scanned signatures will produce page 1 from the text layer and pages 2–5 from OCR. The status message after conversion tells you how many pages came from each source.

Reading Order

PDF has no concept of "reading order". It stores each piece of text at an x/y coordinate. When a human reads a page, we understand the visual flow: left to right, top to bottom, columns in order, sidebars excluded. A machine has to infer that flow.

This tool uses the order in which PDF.js returns text items, which is usually close to the visual order for simple documents. But it can go wrong:

  • Multi-column layouts — text from the left column may be interleaved with text from the right column, producing sentences that jump back and forth.
  • Sidebars and callouts — a sidebar may appear in the middle of the main text instead of at the end of the page.
  • Tables — cells are read in whatever order they were written to the PDF, which is often not row-by-row.
  • Headers and footers — these are usually stored last on the page, so they end up at the end of the page text rather than at the top and bottom.
  • Footnotes — footnote markers appear inline but the footnote text may be grouped with the bottom of the page.

For a single-column article or a plain report, the reading order is usually correct. For complex layouts, expect to do some manual clean-up.

Encoding and Special Characters

PDFs can use several different encodings for text. Most modern PDFs use Unicode, which covers virtually every character in every language. Older PDFs may use custom encodings tied to a specific font.

When the encoding is Unicode, accented characters, currency symbols, and non-Latin scripts come through correctly. When the encoding is a custom font mapping — common in old PDFs and in PDFs that embed subset fonts — some characters may be replaced with the wrong glyph or with a placeholder like ? or ■.

Ligatures are another common issue. A PDF may store the visual ligature "ﬁ" as a single glyph. Some PDFs also provide the underlying "f" and "i" characters; others do not. When they are missing, "ﬁ" stays as "ﬁ" in the output instead of becoming "fi".

Line Breaks and Whitespace

The output preserves line breaks as they appear in the PDF. This is intentional — it makes the text easier to compare against the original — but it means you will often see:

  • Hard line breaks in the middle of a sentence, where the PDF wrapped the line.
  • Extra blank lines between paragraphs, or no blank lines at all, depending on how the PDF was authored.
  • Hyphenated words split across lines, like "docu-\nment".
  • Multiple spaces where the PDF used tabs or column alignment.

A light cleanup pass collapses runs of three or more spaces down to two, and reduces runs of four or more newlines to two. Beyond that, the text is left as-is so you can decide how to reformat it.

Scanned PDFs and OCR — What to Expect

When a page has no usable text layer, the tool renders it at 3× scale and sends it to Tesseract.js, an OCR engine that runs entirely in your browser. The OCR is trained on English text and works well on clean, high-contrast scans of printed material.

OCR quality depends heavily on the source image:

  • Good scans (300 DPI or higher, straight, well-lit, black text on white paper) typically produce 95%+ accurate text.
  • Medium scans (grey backgrounds, slight skew, moderate resolution) usually produce readable text with some errors that need fixing.
  • Poor scans (low resolution, heavy skew, handwritten text, faded ink, complex backgrounds) produce text that needs substantial correction.

OCR also loses all formatting. Bold, italic, headings, and paragraph structure are gone — you get a stream of words in reading order, with whatever line breaks Tesseract detected.

Real Limitations You Should Know

  • No layout preservation. Tables, columns, sidebars, and images are not represented. The output is a flat stream of text.
  • No formatting. Bold, italic, underline, font size, and colour are all lost. Only the characters survive.
  • No images. This is a text-only extraction. Embedded images are not saved or described.
  • No hyperlinks. A link in the PDF becomes its visible text only. The target URL is not preserved as a link.
  • Encrypted PDFs cannot be read. If the PDF requires a password to open, the tool cannot process it. Remove the password first.
  • OCR is English-only by default. Other languages will produce poor results because the OCR engine is loaded with the English trained data. The tool does not currently offer language selection.
  • Very large PDFs may be slow. OCR is computationally heavy. A 100-page scanned document can take several minutes. The progress bar shows where you are.
  • Character accuracy is not guaranteed. Even on clean text layers, unusual fonts or broken encodings can produce wrong characters. On OCR output, some errors are normal.

Privacy — Client-Side Extraction

PDF parsing uses the open-source PDF.js library, and OCR uses Tesseract.js. Both run entirely in your browser. Your PDF is read into memory and never transmitted. The only external resources loaded are the libraries themselves from a CDN. After the page has loaded once, you can disconnect from the internet and the tool will continue to work — including OCR.

Practical Uses

Feeding text to an AI tool: Extract the text from a PDF, copy it, and paste it into a chatbot or summariser. The plain text format is exactly what those tools expect.

Searching a document collection: Extract text from a folder of PDFs and use your operating system's search to find phrases across all of them.

Recovering text from a scanned contract: Run OCR on the scan, then fix any errors manually. Faster than retyping the whole document.

Data analysis: Extract text from reports, then run word frequency, sentiment, or keyword analysis on the plain text.

PDF to TXT Questions

Is my data safe?

Yes. All text extraction and OCR happen locally in your browser. Your PDF is never uploaded to any server. You can disconnect from the internet after the page loads and the tool will continue to work.

How does the tool handle scanned PDFs?

Page by page. For each page, the tool first tries the embedded text layer. If the extracted text is too short or too sparse, it renders that page as an image and runs Tesseract.js OCR on it. This means a PDF that mixes typed and scanned pages is handled correctly — each page uses whichever method works for it.

Why is the reading order sometimes wrong?

PDF stores text at coordinates, not in reading order. The tool uses the order PDF.js returns text items, which is close to visual order for simple documents but can be wrong for multi-column layouts, sidebars, and tables. There is no reliable way to infer reading order without understanding the layout, which this tool does not attempt.

Are special characters and accents preserved?

Usually yes, if the PDF uses Unicode encoding. Older PDFs that use custom font encodings may produce wrong characters or placeholders like ? or ■. This is a limitation of the source PDF, not the converter.

Why are there hard line breaks in the middle of sentences?

Because the PDF wrapped the line at that point, and the converter preserves the line breaks so you can compare the output against the original. If you want flowing paragraphs instead, you can join lines in a text editor after extraction.

Will formatting like bold and italics be preserved?

No. Plain text has no concept of bold or italic. The converter extracts the characters only. If you need formatting, use the PDF to DOCX converter instead, which preserves headings and paragraph structure.

Are images extracted from the PDF?

No. This is a text-only tool. Images embedded in the PDF are not saved or described. If you need the images, extract them with a dedicated PDF image extractor.

How accurate is the OCR?

On clean, high-contrast scans of printed English text, OCR is typically 95% accurate or better. On low-resolution scans, skewed pages, handwritten text, or complex backgrounds, accuracy drops significantly. Always proofread OCR output before using it.

Does OCR support languages other than English?

No. The tool loads the English trained data for Tesseract.js. Other languages will produce poor results. Language selection is not currently offered.

Can I convert a password-protected PDF?

No. The tool can only read PDFs that open without a password. If your PDF requires a password, remove the protection first using a dedicated PDF tool, then convert it here.

Why is the output so slow for scanned PDFs?

OCR is computationally heavy. Each page is rendered at high resolution and then analysed by the OCR engine, all in your browser. A 50-page scanned document can take several minutes. The progress bar shows which page is being processed.

What is the difference between this tool and the PDF to Markdown converter?

This tool produces plain text with no structure — no headings, no lists, no paragraph markers. The PDF to Markdown converter tries to detect headings and lists and produces structured Markdown. If you want structure, use Markdown. If you want raw text, use this tool.
