How WebP to JPG Conversion Works

The converter hands your WebP file to the browser's built-in WebP decoder, reads the decoded pixels, draws them onto an HTML canvas, and then encodes the canvas as a JPEG. This is a decode-then-re-encode pipeline, and it runs entirely on your device.

Everything happens locally. Your file is never uploaded, and the output is written straight to your device through a Blob URL.

The Transparency Problem — JPEG Has No Alpha Channel

This is the most important thing to understand about converting WebP to JPG. WebP supports transparency. JPEG does not. There is no alpha channel in a JPEG file, and the format has no mechanism for expressing "this pixel is partially or fully see-through".

When a WebP image has transparent areas — the background of a logo, the cut-out edge of a product photo, the shadow around a sticker — those pixels cannot be carried into the JPG as-is. They must be replaced with something. The two options are:

  • Fill with a solid colour. Every transparent pixel is replaced with that colour. This is what this converter does.
  • Leave them undefined. The JPEG encoder would either write black or garbage. This is not useful.

What Background Does Droplet Use?

Droplet fills every transparent pixel with solid white (#FFFFFF) before the JPEG is encoded. This is the same convention used by every mainstream image editor (Photoshop, GIMP, Affinity Photo) when flattening a transparent layer for JPEG export.

The practical effect: if your WebP logo has a transparent background, the resulting JPG logo will have a white background. If your WebP product photo has a transparent background, the JPG will show a white background behind the product.

There is no option to change the fill colour in this version. If you need a different background — black, a brand colour, a photo — you would need to composite the WebP onto that background in an image editor before converting.

If you must keep transparency, choose the PNG option in the Output Format dropdown. PNG supports an alpha channel and preserves transparent pixels exactly.

When Transparency Does Not Matter

Most WebP photos in the wild are fully opaque. A photograph taken by a camera does not have transparency, and neither does a screenshot of a webpage or a saved chart. For these files, the transparency issue is irrelevant — every pixel already has a colour, and the JPG output looks exactly like the source.

The transparency problem only matters for images that were specifically authored with transparency: logos, icons, stickers, cut-out product shots, graphics on a design canvas.

Quality Control — What the Setting Actually Does

JPEG is a lossy format. Every time an image is saved as JPEG, the encoder discards some visual information to reduce file size. The quality setting controls how aggressive that discard is.

Setting Quality Value Typical Use
High (Smaller) 0.70 Email attachments, quick previews, thumbnails, mobile screens
Very High 0.85 General use, sharing, archiving — the default
Maximum (Larger) 0.95 Printing, detailed photos, further editing

At 0.95, the difference from the original is usually invisible to the eye. At 0.70, you may notice slight blurring around sharp edges, faint banding in smooth gradients, and softening of fine text. For photos viewed at normal size, 0.85 is a good balance.

Note that re-encoding an image that was already a lossy WebP introduces a second generation of lossy compression. WebP lossy artefacts and JPEG lossy artefacts do not cancel out — they compound. For the best result, use Maximum quality when the source was already lossy.

File Size Differences

WebP and JPEG are both lossy formats with similar compression technology, but WebP generally achieves smaller files at the same visual quality. Typical comparisons:

  • WebP to JPG at the same quality level: the JPG is usually 20–40% larger.
  • WebP to JPG at the maximum quality: the JPG can be 2–3× larger.
  • Lossless WebP to JPG: the JPG will usually be much smaller, because JPEG is lossy and discards information while lossless WebP preserves everything.

The practical implication: converting your WebP library to JPG will increase your total storage footprint. The trade-off is universal compatibility. If file size matters more than compatibility, keep the WebP files. If compatibility matters more, use JPG and accept the larger size.

Resolution and Image Dimensions

The output JPG uses the source dimensions by default. A 1920 × 1080 WebP becomes a 1920 × 1080 JPG. Aspect ratio is always preserved.

The Image Size option caps the longest edge at 1920, 1280, or 800 pixels. Original Size keeps the native resolution. When downscaling, the browser uses high-quality interpolation.

Metadata

WebP files can carry EXIF and XMP metadata: camera model, GPS coordinates, capture date, editing history. This converter does not preserve any of it. The decoded pixels are drawn onto a canvas, and the JPEG encoder writes no EXIF or XMP chunks. The output is a clean JPG with pixel data only.

This is good for privacy — a photo shared online will not leak its GPS location — but it means any descriptive metadata attached to the original is lost.

Practical Examples

Converting a WebP photo for a print service: Upload the WebP, choose Original Size, Maximum quality, and JPG. Since photos have no transparency, the white fill does not apply. The output is accepted by any print service.

Converting a WebP logo for an old CMS: Upload the WebP logo, choose Original Size, Very High quality, and JPG. The transparent background becomes white. If the logo will be placed on a coloured background, it will show a white box around it — in that case, use PNG instead.

Reducing size for email: Upload the WebP photos, choose 1280px, High quality. The output is a small JPG suitable for email attachments.

Converting a screenshot for a documentation platform: Upload the WebP screenshot, choose Original Size, Very High quality, JPG. Screenshots are usually opaque, so no background fill is applied.

Converting a transparent icon where the white fill is acceptable: Upload the WebP icon, choose Original Size, Maximum quality, JPG. The icon appears on a white square. Use this when the icon will be placed on a white surface anyway.

WebP to JPG Questions

Is my data safe?

Yes. All conversion happens locally in your browser. Your WebP files are never uploaded to any server. You can disconnect from the internet after the page loads and the tool will continue to work.

Why convert WebP to JPG?

WebP is a modern format with excellent compression, but it is not yet supported by all devices, operating systems, and applications. JPG is universally supported and works everywhere — email clients, office software, print services, and legacy systems.

Will I lose image quality?

JPEG is a lossy format, so some quality is lost. At the Very High setting (0.85), the loss is imperceptible for most images. At Maximum (0.95), the loss is minimal. At High (0.70), you may notice slight softening around sharp edges and in fine text.

What happens to transparent areas in my WebP?

JPEG does not support transparency, so transparent pixels cannot be preserved. Droplet fills every transparent pixel with solid white (#FFFFFF) before encoding the JPG. If your WebP logo had a transparent background, the output JPG will have a white background.

Can I choose a different background colour for transparency?

Not in this version. The fill colour is fixed at white. If you need a different background — black, a brand colour, or a photo — composite the WebP onto that background in an image editor before converting.

How do I keep transparency?

Choose the PNG option in the Output Format dropdown. PNG supports an alpha channel and preserves transparent pixels exactly. The trade-off is a larger file size than JPG.

Will the JPG be larger or smaller than the WebP?

Usually larger. At the same visual quality, a JPG is typically 20–40% larger than the equivalent WebP. At Maximum quality, the JPG can be 2–3× larger. If the source was a lossless WebP, the JPG will usually be much smaller because JPEG is lossy.

Which quality setting should I use?

Very High (0.85) is the default and works well for most uses. Choose Maximum (0.95) if you plan to print or edit the JPG further. Choose High (0.70) if file size matters more than detail, for example when emailing.

Does re-encoding an already-lossy WebP cause extra quality loss?

Yes. A lossy WebP has already discarded some information, and JPEG discards more. The two rounds of lossy compression compound. For the best result, use Maximum quality when the source was already lossy.

Are metadata like GPS and camera info preserved?

No. EXIF, XMP, and container metadata are stripped during conversion. The canvas API works with pixels only, and the JPEG encoder writes no metadata chunks. This is good for privacy but means descriptive metadata is lost.

Can I resize the image during conversion?

Yes. Choose an Image Size option to cap the longest edge at 1920, 1280, or 800 pixels. Original Size keeps the native resolution. Aspect ratio is always preserved.

What is the maximum file size?

About 50 MB per image. Beyond that, browser memory and rendering speed become limiting factors. For very large images, resize them first or convert them in a smaller batch.
